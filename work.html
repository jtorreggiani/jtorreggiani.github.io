2020 — present

Building my own things

In 2026 I left Square to co-found Serspace Labs — the newest chapter, and the one I'm writing now. It's the latest in a thread that started in 2020, when I co-founded the Sustainable Progress and Equality Collective, an open learning collective that has channeled over $300K into open-source projects and career training for underrepresented communities — including $80K of my own. A two-year partnership with the RAND Corporation's Center to Advance Racial Equity Policy produced microcredentials, socio-cultural gaming research, and an intern program that placed half its participants into tech roles.

Alongside both, since 2023 I've run Integrative R&D, a consulting practice for teams facing meaningful technical challenges.

2020 — 2026

Scale at Square

Before that, five and a half years as a staff engineer in Square's Payroll domain taught me what software looks like when real businesses depend on it every pay cycle. I led a team of three to rebuild the Run Payroll page from legacy JavaScript to TypeScript React and Protobuf APIs, taking page loads from more than 20 seconds to under 3 for our largest sellers. I also led the Free Trial Experiment, introducing A/B testing to the domain and driving a roughly 20% increase in monthly recurring revenue.

The work I'm proudest of there wasn't code: as Bay Area chair of Square's Neurodiversity Community, I helped grow it from 100 members to 1,000, leading events on workplace inclusion and mental health.

2018 — 2020

Leading teams at Artemis

At Artemis, an agtech company building cultivation management software, I got to combine engineering with the sustainability questions that drew me to this work in the first place. I spearheaded breaking up the Rails monolith behind a JSON:API, led development of a React Native mobile app for growers, and published an open-source state management library used across our web and mobile clients.

It's also where I learned to lead. As engineering manager I ran a team of ten — interviews, mentorship, performance reviews, and the daily work of coordinating priorities between product, customer success, and sales. I came away convinced that most "technical" problems on a team are really communication problems wearing a disguise.

2013 — 2017

Learning the craft

In 2015 I joined Verba, an edtech startup in San Francisco, as a core contributor to the frontend of its flagship course-materials product. I led the migration from Backbone to React, built a student-facing portal, and integrated our billing with a major enterprise student information system — automating reconciliation work that moved millions of dollars through university accounts receivable departments. Verba was acquired by VitalSource while I was there, my first time seeing a startup through to an exit.

The freelance years around it taught me range. I built an automated, FDA-compliant interactive voice response system for a Fortune 500 pharmaceutical company's product release — delivered two months early and $15K under budget — and, at the other end of the spectrum, a Ruby GIS application that let volunteers in Plymouth, England map the planting and health of trees in their city's central park. That citizen-science project is still one of my favorites: ordinary people with phones, producing real spatial data about their own neighborhood.

2012 — 2013

Construction sites to code

It all started somewhere unexpected: my first real job out of college had nothing to do with software. I was a project management assistant at a construction firm in Poughkeepsie, New York, working on multimillion-dollar projects — at one point writing the EPA-mandated spill prevention plan that protected an endangered turtle species living next to one of our sites. A history degree turns out to be decent preparation for construction paperwork: both are mostly about understanding how systems of people, rules, and materials actually behave.

What I kept noticing was how much of the work was tracked on paper and whiteboards. So I started building tools: a web-based inventory system with barcode tags, a scheduling system for moving crews between sites, a drag-and-drop board for labor allocation. Nobody asked me to become a programmer — the problems did. In 2013 I made it official with a back-end web development program at General Assembly in New York City, and the construction firm became my first software client.

If you want the chronological ledger — titles, dates, and bullet points — that lives on my resume.
